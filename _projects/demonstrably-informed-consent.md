Qian Ma, **Aditya Majumdar**, Sarah Rajtmajer, Brett Frischmann. *Under review.*
[arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.09518)

Online, consent usually means one click at the end of a long privacy policy that almost nobody reads. Recent legal scholarship argues that consent should only count if the person can show they understood the key terms.

We tested whether small additions to the consent flow, which we call pedagogical friction, can help. We recruited 293 parents of children aged 3 to 8 to review the privacy policy of an educational app under one of six conditions that changed how the policy was presented and paced. Each parent then took a six-question comprehension quiz, and some conditions offered a second review and retake to those who did not pass.

Different designs helped at different stages, and a second review let many parents reach the comprehension threshold, at the cost of extra time. The most striking finding: when consent was not tied to passing the quiz, 97.3% of parents who scored below the threshold agreed anyway.

I helped build the experiment's web implementation and the backend that stored participant responses.
