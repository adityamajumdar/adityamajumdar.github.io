**Aditya Majumdar**, Wenbo Zhang, Kashvi Prawal, Amulya Yadav. *ACM FAccT 2026*, Montreal.
[Paper](https://doi.org/10.1145/3805689.3812387) | [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.14829)

The number of AI for social impact projects tripled between 2018 and 2023, from about 170 to over 600. Yet a [2024 McKinsey report](https://www.mckinsey.com/~/media/mckinsey/business%20functions/quantumblack/our%20insights/ai%20for%20social%20good/2024/ai-for-social-good-improving-lives-and-protecting-the-planet-v2.pdf) found that 72% of them never leave the research and development stage. We wanted to understand why, from the point of view of the researchers doing the work.

We interviewed 26 researchers, mostly in U.S. academia, about 38 projects (including five of our own) in conservation, public health, agriculture, food security, social justice, and public safety. Many had built something that worked technically but never reached the people it was meant for.

## What gets in the way

We grouped the barriers into four kinds. A project usually has to get past all of them.

| Barrier | What it looks like in practice |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Structural and organizational** | Academia rewards frequent papers, not slow field work. Partner staff take on AI work on top of their real jobs. Grants rarely pay for maintenance, and data agreements can take months. |
| **Communication** | Researchers and partners mean different things by "AI." Progress often depends on one person inside the organization who can translate between the two. |
| **Collaboration** | Projects stall without decision makers on board. Partners worry that researchers will publish and leave. Leadership may say yes while frontline staff see no use for the tool. |
| **Operational** | Priorities, policies, and staff change. Data turns out to be missing or too sensitive to share. University labs are not set up to maintain software for years. |

One story captures the gap well. On a wildlife conservation project, senior officials were excited about the AI tool, but the rangers who would use it said they needed better equipment, not AI.

## What helps

**Start with a quick win.** Researchers who reached deployment often delivered something small and useful first, such as a simple analysis of the partner's own data, before the main modeling work. This built trust and helped them understand the real problem.

**Work through people the partner already trusts.** Collaborators from public health or social work who know the partner can speed things up, although this can also shut out newer researchers without those connections.

**Fund the exploratory phase.** Small grants that let researchers and partners test the fit before committing years of work reduce costly mismatches later.

**Change what counts as research.** Universities can recognize deployed systems and open-source tools as research contributions, and can share the load of data agreements and partner outreach through central offices.

The communities these projects serve are rarely a market that companies will build for. If research is going to reach them, the incentives around that research need to change. We hope the paper works as a practical guide for researchers and partner organizations starting this kind of collaboration.
